# Case 006 — 340,000 euros in donations, one pension law, and the counterevidence

## Question

What can the public record prove about the relationship between DVAG’s 2026 party donations, the lobbying positions of its member association BDV and the final private-pension reform?

## Verified facts

The Bundestag’s large-donation register records five DVAG donations totalling **EUR 340,000**: EUR 100,000 each to CDU and SPD; EUR 50,000 each to FDP and Bündnis 90/Die Grünen on 27 January 2026; and another EUR 40,000 to the CDU on 2 February.

DVAG’s Lobby Register entry reports **EUR 490,001–500,000** in lobbying expenditure for 2025, two directly entrusted lobbyists, and memberships including the Bundesverband Deutscher Vermögensberater (BDV), Wirtschaftsrat der CDU and Wirtschaftsforum der SPD. DVAG’s own current entry lists no concrete regulatory project.

BDV registered a concrete project on the Altersvorsorgereformgesetz. Its central objection concerned the planned spreading of acquisition and distribution costs over the full savings period, which it described as commercially difficult for advisers.

## Demand-to-law matrix

| BDV position | Final outcome | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Reconsider full-period spreading of acquisition and distribution costs, or narrow its scope. | Full-period spreading remained a core rule. | **Not adopted** |
| Avoid combining cost spreading with a strict product-cost cap. | The committee tightened the standard-product effective-cost cap from the government draft’s 1.5% to 1.0%. | **Opposite direction** |
| Preserve ordinary advice and distribution channels. | The committee stated that all distribution and advice channels should remain possible, without making advice mandatory. | **Partly reflected** |
| Preserve annuity and guarantee options. | Those options remained, but were already part of the government proposal before the donations. | **Aligned, but pre-existing** |

## Finding

The public record proves significant political spending, a direct organisational link between DVAG and BDV and proximity to an economically relevant legislative process. It **does not prove a purchased decision or causal effect of the donations**. The strongest observable test cuts against that claim: key intermediary demands were not adopted, and one consumer-cost safeguard became stricter during the parliamentary process.

## Safe publication line

> DVAG donated EUR 340,000 to four parties while a member association represented intermediary interests in the pension reform. The available documents show institutional proximity, but not a purchased decision. Several central association demands were rejected or met with stricter cost controls.

# Case 007 — Amazon, direct awards and the new UVgO

## Question

Which publicly stated stakeholder demands reappear in the 2026 UVgO draft, and what can be said about their origin?

## Verified facts

Amazon registered an interest-representation project active from December 2024 to January 2026 seeking a substantial increase in direct-award thresholds and new possibilities for direct awards through online marketplaces.

BMWE published a new UVgO draft on 30 June 2026, reducing the regulation from 54 to 24 sections and opening consultation until **28 August 2026**. The wider federal procurement-acceleration policy had already announced a federal direct-award threshold of EUR 50,000.

Other actors, including major research organisations, had also requested increased direct-award thresholds. Their documented requests commonly proposed at least EUR 15,000. Threshold reform therefore cannot responsibly be attributed to Amazon alone.

## Finding

There is a **documented policy overlap** between Amazon’s request and the direction of federal procurement reform: higher direct-award thresholds. That overlap does not establish authorship, exclusivity or improper influence. The evidence currently available to this dossier does not establish that an explicit online-marketplace provision requested by Amazon was adopted in the 2026 draft.

## Counterevidence and alternative explanations

- Higher thresholds were advocated by multiple public-interest and research institutions, not only commercial marketplaces.
- The federal government publicly frames the change as administrative simplification and faster procurement.
- The 2026 draft also includes transparency and corruption-prevention elements, so it is not accurately described as an unqualified adoption of vendor demands.

## Safe publication line

> Amazon publicly sought higher direct-award thresholds and online-marketplace purchasing. The government’s reform moves toward higher thresholds, but that policy had broad support among other organisations. Whether Amazon’s marketplace-specific demand entered the text remains unproven.

# Case 008 — Germany’s GRECO status after the March deadline

## Question

Did Germany submit the progress report requested for 31 March 2026, and what is publicly known about the outstanding recommendations?

## Verified facts

GRECO’s second fifth-round compliance report, published on 8 August 2025, assessed 14 recommendations: **four satisfactorily implemented, six partly implemented and four not implemented**. GRECO concluded that Germany was not in sufficient compliance and requested a progress report by 31 March 2026.

As of the research freeze on 25 July 2026, GRECO’s Germany country page and fifth-round overview still list the second compliance report published on 8 August 2025 as the latest public German fifth-round compliance publication.

## Finding

The post-deadline status is **not publicly verifiable from GRECO’s German publication page**. This is a genuine transparency gap. It does not prove that Germany failed to submit a report: submissions may remain confidential while under review, and publication can depend on procedural steps or national authorisation.

## Outstanding themes named by GRECO

- effective monitoring of integrity standards for top executive functions;
- systematic integrity briefings for ministers and parliamentary state secretaries;
- stronger federal access to information;
- sufficient public disclosure of meetings with lobbyists and third parties;
- clear disclosure of conflicts as they occur and regular public financial-interest declarations;
- stronger screening and proactive internal oversight in federal law enforcement.

## Safe publication line

> GRECO asked Germany to report by 31 March 2026. Nearly four months later, no newer German fifth-round report is publicly listed. That does not prove non-submission, but citizens cannot currently verify the implementation status from the public country record.

## Primary sources

# Case 009 — What Berlin’s procurement pages show, and what they do not

## Question

Can citizens identify low-competition or concentrated procurement patterns from Berlin’s public award notices?

## Verified facts

Berlin publishes ex-post information for defined categories, including restricted procedures without a participation competition above EUR 25,000, negotiated awards without a participation competition above EUR 25,000 under UVgO and certain freely negotiated construction awards above EUR 15,000.

Public detail pages commonly expose the authority, procedure, subject, reference number, supplier, location and execution period. Examples visible in 2026 include architectural planning, structural engineering, cleaning and event-service awards using negotiated procedures without a participation competition.

Those visible pages often do **not** show the award value, number of bids, detailed legal justification, contract modifications or beneficial ownership. A negotiated procedure can be lawful; its mere presence is not evidence of favouritism or corruption.

Nationally, Destatis records 199,334 public awards worth EUR 135.2 billion for 2024. The Berlin pages are therefore only one disclosure layer and should be combined with the national Procurement Publication Service and its open data.

## Finding

Berlin provides a useful public trail, but the commonly visible ex-post pages are insufficient by themselves for a defensible value-for-money or competition assessment. The first publishable finding is a **data-quality and accountability gap**, not a suspect-company list.

## Minimum fields for meaningful scrutiny

1. award value and initial estimate;
2. number of invitations and bids;
3. exact legal basis and justification;
4. contract duration and modifications;
5. machine-readable authority and supplier identifiers;
6. link to the original notice and procurement documents;
7. conflict-of-interest and beneficial-ownership checks where legally publishable.

## Safe publication line

> Berlin publishes who awarded what to whom in many non-open procedures, but key context such as price, bid count and detailed justification is frequently absent from the visible notice. Those omissions prevent citizens from distinguishing an ordinary lawful exception from a pattern worth investigating.

# Case 010 — The Bundestag’s conflict-of-interest data gap

## Question

Can citizens reproducibly connect MPs’ disclosed outside interests with committee roles and related legislative activity?

## Verified facts

Members must disclose specified paid activities, company functions, relevant association roles, holdings and certain future-benefit arrangements. Income for an individual activity is published exactly when it exceeds EUR 1,000 in a month or EUR 3,000 in a calendar year. The disclosures appear on individual biography pages and are updated continuously.

The Bundestag’s Open Data portal provides biographical master data, parliamentary papers and plenary records in machine-readable formats, and named-vote lists in spreadsheets. It does not advertise one integrated, versioned dataset that connects current outside-interest disclosures with committee membership, reporting roles, hearings, speeches and votes.

The private-pension hearing provides a useful counterexample to careless screening: the hearing was chaired by Anja Karliczek, whose current published disclosure lists hospitality-sector and nonprofit roles, not a disclosed finance or insurance interest. A policy role alone is therefore not evidence of a private-interest overlap.

## Finding

Germany has substantive disclosure rules, but public oversight remains technically fragmented. A reproducible monitor must combine frequently changing biography pages with separate committee, document and voting sources, then apply conservative human judgment. This increases error risk and makes change history harder to audit.

## Safe publication line

> The Bundestag discloses outside interests and offers extensive parliamentary open data, but it does not provide one versioned machine-readable dataset connecting those interests to policy roles. That fragmentation makes systematic, correction-friendly public oversight unnecessarily difficult.

# Case 011 — From audit finding to verified implementation

## Question

What did Parliament require after the Bundesrechnungshof’s 2024 findings, and which implementation claims can the public currently verify?

## Verified parliamentary follow-through

The Bundestag’s committee report 21/2595 records concrete actions and reporting deadlines. Five high-public-interest examples form the initial tracker:

| Audit item | Parliamentary action | Deadline | Status at research freeze |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Complete overview of payments to international organisations | Government must maintain an automated, centrally managed overview from 2026; first status report requested. | 31 Mar 2026 | **Deadline passed; report not located in the public sources reviewed** |
| Green federal bonds | BMF asked to improve criteria and impact evidence; committee report text establishes the dispute and follow-up obligation. | See committee decision/reporting trail | **Implementation not publicly verified in this dossier** |
| Waterway project prioritisation | Updated report must incorporate 2026 budget decisions and project prioritisation. | 1 Aug 2026 | **Due soon** |
| Delayed station programmes | BMV must address programme failures and report on corrective action. | 31 Aug 2026 | **Open** |
| Cash-register inspections | BMF must pursue common objectives with Länder, improve consistent execution and report. | 31 Aug 2026 | **Open** |

## Material facts behind the tracker

- The audit found EUR 8.7 billion in federal payments to international organisations for 2022, while the existing foreign-office view covered EUR 6.8 billion; Parliament requested a reliable central overview from 2026.
- For green federal bonds, impact indicators directly supported only 29% of assigned expenditure according to the audit, while BMF disputed the audit’s standard and pointed to market practice, transparency and safety discounts.
- For station programmes, only EUR 84 million had been used by the end of 2023 against a planned EUR 326 million implementation level; the ministry cited funding, planning, staffing and external constraints.
- Cash-register inspections reached only 3–8% of the intended level from 2018 to 2023, according to the audit; Parliament requested action with the Länder and a report by 31 August 2026.

## Finding

Parliament did not merely “take note”: it created a dated accountability trail. Yet acceptance of an audit finding is not implementation. The publishable public-interest question is now simple and falsifiable: **Where are the requested reports, and what independently verifiable change do they demonstrate?**

## Safe publication line

> Parliament set concrete 2026 reporting deadlines after major federal audit findings. One deadline in this initial tracker has passed and others are imminent. Until the reports and underlying evidence are public, implementation should be labelled “not publicly verified,” not “completed” or “ignored.”

# Editorial conclusion

The six cases do not support one sweeping claim that German politics is corrupt. They support six narrower findings:

1. money, memberships and policy proximity can be documented without proving a transaction;
2. policy overlap must be tested against other advocates and drafting history;
3. overdue public status information is a transparency gap, not proof of failure;
4. procurement oversight is limited when price and competition data are missing;
5. disclosure can be legally strong yet technically difficult to audit;
6. parliamentary acceptance of an audit finding is not the same as verified implementation.

That is the publication standard: **interesting enough to matter, disciplined enough to survive scrutiny.**

## Publication decision

- **Ready for independent editorial review:** all six briefs.
- **Safe for immediate publication as methodology/status briefs:** Cases 008–011, provided their unresolved status is preserved.
- **Hold named-actor investigative publication pending right of reply:** Cases 006 and 007.
- **No case currently supports an allegation of bribery, corruption, favouritism or unlawful influence.**
