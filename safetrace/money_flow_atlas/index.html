Conclusion

Germany has enormous wealth and a large welfare state. The core problem is not simply “too little money”.

Wealth ownership is highly concentrated, capital returns favour households that already own market assets, redistribution is spread across pensions, insurance, services and subsidies rather than only poverty relief, and some targeted benefits still fail to reach eligible people. The solution is to make distributional impact visible per euro and automatically route support to eligible households before adding more complexity.

03 · Why doesn't the money reach the poorest?

Four mechanisms we can actually observe.

1 · Starting ownership

People cannot compound assets they do not own.

The bottom half starts with only a small share of household net wealth. Wealth inequality therefore reproduces itself even without any illegal behaviour.

2 · Different portfolios

The return engine rewards asset ownership differently.

The Bundesbank reports that poorer households hold financial assets mainly in deposits and insurance claims, while capital-market investments matter far more for wealthy households.

3 · Broad, not poverty-only redistribution

A big social budget is not the same as a poverty-targeted budget.

Germany redistributes heavily across the life course through pensions and social insurance. We need to measure who ultimately benefits from each euro instead of inferring it from the ministry name.

4 · Access friction

Eligibility does not guarantee receipt.

One concrete 2026 analysis found only around 18% of eligible 6–14-year-olds and 12% of 15–17-year-olds in the analysed groups used the participation benefit. That is a routing failure.

04 · What I would change

Optimize the system for outcomes, not programme count.

The policy choice remains democratic. The software's job is to make trade-offs, beneficiaries and missed people impossible to hide.

1

Automatic entitlement before new programmes

Where government already has enough verified data, calculate likely eligibility, pre-fill the claim and ask for the minimum missing evidence. The target metric is eligible people actually reached.

2

Give every public euro a Distribution Score

For every transfer, subsidy and tax expenditure: who receives it by income decile and wealth decile? What share reaches the bottom 20%? What is unknown? A €77.8bn subsidy envelope should be inspectable measure by measure, not treated as one political number.

3

Rank reforms by poverty-gap reduction per euro

Run proper microsimulation before claiming an impact. Compare net household gains, fiscal cost, work incentives, housing effects and behavioural responses. IAB/ifo already demonstrate why benefit design cannot be judged from headline spending alone.

4

Do not only transfer income — build assets at the bottom

Test matched savings, child/citizen capital accounts or other broad asset-building mechanisms alongside income support. This attacks the ownership gap that cash transfers alone do not remove. This is a policy hypothesis to evaluate, not a proven optimal design.

Next move

Build the first end-to-end distribution experiment.

Start where evidence already shows a routing failure: choose one existing benefit, estimate the eligible population, show reached vs missed, calculate the unclaimed value, identify the minimum data needed for automatic/pre-filled access, then measure the before/after take-up rate.

After that, attach the same beneficiary-incidence layer to subsidies, tax expenditures and procurement. Then SafeTrace becomes a genuine “where does the money go, who benefits, who is missed, and what should change next?” system.
